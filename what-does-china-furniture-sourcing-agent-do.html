What Does a China Furniture Sourcing Agent Actually Do?

"Sourcing agent" gets used loosely, but the actual job covers a specific, fairly consistent set of tasks between a buyer's spec sheet and a landed container — worth understanding clearly before deciding whether you need one.

Finding and vetting factories

An agent maintains relationships with a network of factories and matches a buyer's spec against the right one — checking export licensing, production capacity and quality history before recommending a supplier, rather than the buyer researching cold.

Negotiating on the buyer's behalf

A good agent negotiates FOB pricing directly with the factory, transparently, so the buyer sees the real factory cost plus an agreed agent fee — distinct from a trading company that resells at an undisclosed markup.

Managing samples and production

The agent coordinates sample rounds, tracks production against the agreed timeline, and flags issues early rather than leaving the buyer to chase a factory directly across a language and time zone gap.

Inspection, consolidation and freight

Independent quality inspection, consolidating multiple factory orders into one shipment, and coordinating export customs and freight booking round out the role — effectively acting as the buyer's on-the-ground team in China for the full order lifecycle.

What the job looks like across one order

The role is easier to judge as a sequence than as a definition. In the first week or two it is mostly research and shortlisting: reading your spec, working out which factories can genuinely produce it, and coming back with a shortlist and indicative pricing. Then it becomes negotiation and sampling — arguing FOB terms, arranging a pre-production sample, and getting your sign-off.

From there the centre of gravity shifts to production tracking and logistics: checking the order is actually on the line when the factory says it is, scheduling inspection points, combining output from several workshops into one shipment, and preparing the export documents. In practice, more of the job is coordination and follow-up than it is hard negotiation.

What an agent is not

An agent is not a trading company. A trading company buys from the factory and resells to you, so its margin is inside the price you see. An agent works for you and is paid for that work, which is why the factory price and the fee should be visible as separate numbers.

An agent is also not your designer, your structural engineer or your compliance consultant. They can flag that a fabric will not meet contract-grade specification or that a finish will not survive a coastal climate, but the specification and the design decisions remain yours. Treating an agent as a design service usually ends with a furniture package that matches the brief only loosely.

What you still own as the buyer

Delegating sourcing does not delegate accountability. You are still the person approving the specification, agreeing the budget, signing off the sample, and accepting the risk on delivery dates. The most common source of friction on a sourcing engagement is not a factory problem — it is a brief that was never fully locked down before production started, leaving the agent to interpret gaps.

Where the time actually goes

Buyers often assume the value of an agent sits in price negotiation. In practice, most of the hours go into the unglamorous parts: chasing a production schedule, catching a finish mismatch at the mid-line inspection, and making sure the commercial invoice and packing list agree with the bill of lading before the container sails.
