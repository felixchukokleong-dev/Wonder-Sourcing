Commercial Furniture Sourcing: The Complete Buyer's Guide

Offices, restaurants, retail floors and multi-site rollouts are bought to survive daily public use and to land before a fit-out date. This commercial furniture sourcing guide covers contract-grade specification, Foshan factory selection, MOQ, compliance, inspection and freight — end to end.

What "commercial" changes about sourcing

Commercial furniture is not residential furniture with a business buyer attached. It is specified to survive usage cycles that a private home never reaches — eight to ten hours of public sitting, daily wipe-downs, chairs dragged across hard floors — and it is bought against a fit-out or opening date that does not move once the lease is signed. Those two facts change everything upstream: which factory qualifies, what grade the specification names, how inspection is scoped and how freight is scheduled.

The third difference is who signs off. A residential order has one buyer; a commercial order usually has an interior designer holding the look, an operator or facilities lead holding the durability requirement, and someone holding the budget. When nobody owns the final specification document, the gaps between those three positions are exactly where projects go wrong — which is why nominating one spec owner before anything is quoted is the first practical step.

The four commercial categories, and where each is made

Commercial work splits into four streams, each with its own centre of gravity in Foshan. Office and co-working furniture — task chairs, desking, storage — sits largely in Nanhai's Guicheng district, one of China's largest office-chair clusters. Restaurant and café seating runs through the upholstery workshops around Shunde, where stackable, linkable and bar-height frames are everyday production. Retail fit-out is casegoods and display joinery out of Lecong. Hotel and serviced-apartment work is its own discipline — covered separately in our hospitality furniture sourcing guide.

The practical value of knowing the cluster is consolidation: a single office-plus-café project can be split across the right specialist factories and still move as one container, rather than forcing one factory outside its category to do everything.

Specifying contract grade — and saying so in writing

Grade is the single biggest cost-and-durability lever, and a factory quoting without a stated contract standard will quietly price a residential build. The numbers worth naming in the brief: fabric rub counts (Martindale 30,000+ for public seating, 50,000+ for transport-adjacent or 24-hour environments), high-resilience foam density for seating that must hold its shape through thousands of compressions, reinforced or metal-reinforced joinery at the points that fail first — back-leg-to-seat-frame, arm-to-frame — and floor and wall protection on anything that moves.

Compliance is specified per destination, not chosen once: fire behaviour (CAL 117 for US projects, BS 5852 or EN standards in Europe), cleanability for healthcare and food-service surfaces, and labelling that matches the certificate. Contract grade typically prices 15–35% above a residential equivalent — against furniture that has to be replaced in year two if you skip it. If the project needs a sustainability claim, name the certification in the spec as well: FSC or an equivalent scheme is increasingly a client requirement rather than a nice-to-have.

MOQ, standardisation and how commercial buyers beat the minimum

Foshan factories price in minimums per SKU, not per order — commonly 50–100 units for seating, 30–50 for tables or casegoods, lower on stock designs. Commercial buyers are structurally better placed than retail ones to meet these: the same task chair needed across four offices, or the same café chair across a rollout, is one SKU at volume rather than five odd quantities. Standardising the range before the first order is the move that turns MOQ from an obstacle into a price advantage, and it is also what makes later phases cheap — see how furniture MOQ actually works for the unit-price curves.

Splitting a project across categories but keeping SKUs consistent also lets several factories consolidate into one shipment. That is the mechanism behind the freight saving, and it needs to be designed into the order, not discovered at the port.

Vetting a factory for contract work specifically

General manufacturing capability is not the filter — contract experience is. Ask which commercial projects the factory has supplied before, to which markets, and whether they can show pieces in service rather than in a showroom. Check capacity honestly: a factory running retail volume has different scheduling behaviour than one used to project waves, and a fit-out does not tolerate "two weeks later". Verify how much is made in-house versus sub-contracted, because a sub-contracted upholstery bay is precisely where contract-grade foam and seam standards get lost. Cost drivers: what actually moves the commercial number

On commercial orders the unit price is rarely the whole story. Freight is charged by volume, so upholstered seating costs disproportionately more to ship than compact casegoods — and that ratio can change which items are worth buying overseas at all. Installation and assembly are not in the factory price. Spares and a small replacement pool are worth budgeting at 5–8% of the first order, which is far cheaper than a rushed five-piece reorder at full freight a year later. For the full arithmetic — FOB, freight, duty, destination tax and agent fees — run the numbers through the landed cost calculator before committing the budget, not after.

Inspection and compliance before anything loads

Commercial orders fail differently from retail ones: a finish mismatch is annoying in a home showroom, but forty chairs with inconsistent fabric dye is a rejected fit-out. The checkpoints that protect a commercial project are the same three we run on every order — pre-production, mid-line and pre-shipment — scoped to contract criteria: foam density verified against the spec, seam and joinery stress points, rub-count certificates on file, fire labels present and correct. See how the three-stage inspection works. Compliance documents should be assembled before production starts, not at customs — a missing fire certificate at the destination port stops the shipment, and it cannot be fixed by the factory once goods have sailed.

Rollouts, spares and the second order

The commercial order that pays for the sourcing work is usually the second one. A locked specification — written construction details, an approved physical sample retained at the factory, and a fabric reference with the mill named — turns phase two of a rollout into a purchase order instead of a redesign. That discipline is also what gets you priority production slots and better pricing as volume grows; the relationship economics are covered in building long-term factory relationships. Budget spares from the first shipment: frame components and fabric offcuts for future repairs travel free inside the first container and save a separate order later.

Common questions

Common questions.

What is commercial furniture sourcing?

Sourcing furniture for business use — offices, restaurants, retail floors, hotels and clinics — specified to contract grade and bought against project timelines, rather than for private homes. It is defined by usage cycles, compliance requirements and fixed fit-out dates rather than by catalogue style.

How is commercial sourcing different from residential?

Three differences drive everything: grade (fabrics, foam and joinery must survive public-use cycles, not domestic ones), timing (fit-out and opening dates are fixed and penalised), and compliance (fire behaviour, cleanability and labelling must meet the destination market's rules). Residential buying tolerates all three loosely; commercial tolerates none.

What MOQ should I expect on a commercial order?

Typically 50–100 units per seating SKU and 30–50 per table or casegoods SKU in Foshan. Commercial buyers usually beat minimums through standardisation — the same chair or table repeated across sites turns the project into one SKU at real volume, which is also what unlocks better unit pricing and cheaper later phases.

What standards apply to imported commercial furniture?

They follow the destination market: fire behaviour such as CAL 117 for US projects and BS 5852 or EN standards in Europe, cleanability requirements for food-service and healthcare, and labelling that matches the certificates. Sustainability certification like FSC is increasingly written into tenders. Confirm all of it before production starts — none of it can be fixed at the destination port.
