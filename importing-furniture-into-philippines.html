Shipping Furniture to the Philippines: A Buyer's Guide

Manila's condo and BPO office construction boom has made office and residential fit-out furniture one of the steadiest import categories into the Philippines, alongside a growing hospitality sector.

The main entry point

Manila International Container Port (MICP) is the primary gateway for Luzon-bound cargo, with ocean transit from Foshan running roughly 5 to 7 days.

What's commonly imported

Desking, seating and storage for BPO and corporate office developments, bedroom and living room sets for residential condo developers, and casegoods and upholstery for hotel and serviced-apartment projects are the categories we see most.

Reaching other islands

Buyers based in Cebu or Davao should confirm onward domestic transfer timing from Manila, since most South China sailings land at MICP first before goods move on by domestic shipping.

Working around developer deadlines

Condo and office developer handover schedules tend to be tight and fixed, which makes it worth building inspection and freight timing around the fit-out deadline rather than the other way around.

Manila's two container terminals

Manila International Container Port (MICP) and Manila South Harbor sit a few kilometres apart on the same waterfront, and which one your container lands at is decided by the carrier's rotation rather than by preference. MICP takes the majority of furniture imports. Both are inside the city, so the final leg is short — but Metro Manila restricts container trucking on certain roads during daytime hours, which shapes when your delivery can actually move.

For projects in Cebu, Davao or the Visayas, routing through Manila and feeding onward is common, though a direct Cebu call can save a week depending on the carrier's schedule. Ask us to price both routings before booking.

Duty, VAT and the Form E route

The Philippines assesses import duty by HS code on the CIF value, then VAT at 12% on the duty-paid total. Furniture sits in HS Chapter 94. Settle the classification early: the Bureau of Customs can question a declared value, and a valuation challenge is the most common reason a container sits beyond its free time.

Under the ASEAN–China Free Trade Area, a Form E certificate of origin can reduce duty on Chinese-origin furniture. As elsewhere in the region, it must reconcile with the invoice and bill of lading and be issued before shipment — a defective Form E means the standard rate applies at the border.

Registration and clearance practice

Importers must be registered with the Bureau of Customs before the goods arrive, and the registration details have to match the consignee named on the bill of lading. A mismatch between the two is a routine cause of held containers, and it is one of the easiest problems to avoid entirely.

Most buyers work through a Philippine customs broker. The ones who handle furniture regularly know which terminals are backlogged in a given week — information worth asking for before you commit to an inland transport booking.

Standards and documentation quirks

Some categories need permits or clearances from Philippine agencies before release. In furniture orders the usual triggers are anything with electrical components, or goods made from timber species subject to environmental regulation. Packing lists should state HS codes per line, and solid-wood packaging must carry ISPM 15 treatment marks or it will be treated or returned at your cost.

Where first shipments usually slip

Metro Manila's trucking restrictions, valuation queries at the Bureau of Customs, and consignee details that do not match the registration are the three most common causes of delay here. Add the pre-Christmas build-up: port volumes rise sharply from October and clearance slows with them, so if your project has a fixed opening date in the first quarter, ship earlier than the calendar suggests.
