Importing Furniture into Thailand: A Buyer's Guide

Thailand's tourism-driven hospitality sector makes it a steady, high-volume market for furniture imports, with outdoor and resort furniture typically the largest single category shipped in from South China.

The main entry point

Laem Chabang Port is Thailand's main deep-sea container port and typically offers more frequent sailings than Bangkok Port itself, with ocean transit from Foshan running roughly 4 to 6 days.

What's commonly imported

Rattan, wicker and weather-rated outdoor furniture for beach and pool developments dominate demand, alongside hotel casegoods and upholstery for refurbishment projects and office and retail fit-out furniture for Bangkok's commercial developments.

Getting furniture to island resorts

Resort projects on Phuket and Koh Samui typically route through Laem Chabang before onward domestic transport by road or short-sea barge — worth building extra time into the schedule beyond the main ocean transit.

Seasonal planning

Resort renovation and fit-out work tends to concentrate in Thailand's shoulder season between peak tourist periods, which is also when freight demand — and pricing — tends to rise across the region.

Two ports, two different jobs

Laem Chabang, south of Bangkok on the eastern seaboard, handles the overwhelming majority of Thailand's container traffic and is where most furniture imports land. Bangkok Port — still widely called Klong Toey — sits on the Chao Phraya inside the city and occasionally suits smaller consignments bound for central Bangkok, but it is draft-limited and takes fewer, smaller vessels.

From Laem Chabang, inland trucking to a Bangkok warehouse typically adds a day. If your site is in Chiang Mai or the northeast, allow two to three days and expect the container to be destuffed and forwarded by road rather than delivered intact.

Duty, VAT and the Form E route

Thailand applies import duty by HS code to the CIF value, then VAT at 7% on the duty-paid total. Furniture sits in HS Chapter 94, and the rate varies by sub-heading — so classification is worth settling before the first container rather than correcting afterwards.

Under the ASEAN–China Free Trade Area, a Form E certificate of origin can reduce duty on Chinese-origin furniture to nil for many lines. Thai customs is strict about the certificate reconciling with the invoice and bill of lading; discrepancies, late issuance, or a certificate naming a different exporter than the invoice will push the consignment onto the standard rate.

Standards and labelling

Thailand's TISI sets mandatory standards for certain categories, and while most furniture is outside their scope, specific lines can fall in — anything with electrical components, such as lamps or powered recliners, is the usual trigger. Thai labelling rules also require importer details and product information in Thai for many consumer goods.

If your order includes lighting or electrical elements, tell us before production starts so the labelling and compliance work happens at the factory rather than as a surprise at the port.

Who clears your goods

Clearance is normally handled by a licensed Thai customs broker, and most of our buyers already have one. If you don't, we can introduce brokers who handle furniture imports regularly. The practical difference between an experienced broker and a generalist shows up in how quickly a valuation or classification query gets answered — which is exactly when demurrage starts accruing.

Where first shipments usually slip

The patterns are familiar: classification argued at the border because the invoice described goods in commercial language, Form E defects, and inland delivery booked before the container was actually released. A free-time check with your broker — how many days the terminal allows before demurrage starts — is worth doing on every shipment, because demurrage on a forty-foot container climbs quickly.
