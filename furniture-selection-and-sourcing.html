Furniture Selection and Sourcing: From Brief to Container

Two disciplines, one process. Furniture selection is choosing what to buy. Sourcing is making it manufacturable. This guide shows how the two work together to get the right pieces from Foshan to your warehouse on time and at the right price.

Most buyers I meet make the mistake of doing these two things in separate heads. They think about furniture selection — what colours, what styles, what will the interior designer say — and then they think about sourcing — which factory, at what price, when can it ship.

But furniture selection and sourcing are not two separate things. They are one process. The pieces you select are what the factory builds. The factory's capability determines what you can select. If you select a piece that the factory cannot build, delivery slips. If you select a piece that the factory can build quickly, you win.

That is the thesis: successful furniture sourcing is selection and sourcing done as one, in the same room, by the same person who knows the showroom and the factory. We've built Wonder Sourcing around that premise.

Phase 1

Selection: what you are actually buying

Furniture selection is an exercise in constraint. Every showroom carries 200–300 SKUs; your business can only sell five. The question is not which pieces you like — it is which pieces satisfy the brief, hold their value, and can be manufactured reliably.

We narrow the selection using four screens:

  1. Market screen — what is already selling in your showroom, what your competitors carry, what the showroom trends indicate.
  2. Fit screen — does the piece physically fit your space, residential or commercial, and your customer's body?
  3. Margin screen — can the piece be manufactured at a price that still gives you a margin?
  4. Supply screen — can this piece be made by a factory in Guangdong without a custom mould?

Most buyers short-cut to screens two and three. The suppliers who ship pre-fabricated furniture from China to their homes are not the buyers who win. The buyers who win spend time on screens one and four.

Phase 2

Sourcing: turning selection into a production order

Once the selection is narrowed from 300 SKUs to 15, sourcing takes over. The source is not a catalogue. The source is a factory. And the factory doesn't buy designs from a website; it buys them from a brief.

We write the brief in five stages, and each stage has a gate:

  • Tech pack — dimensions, materials, screw torque, face frames, upholstery, and finish swatches. The factory manufacturer reviews this before quoting.
  • Sample approval — the client reviews a working prototype. If we don't approve, tooling is not cut.
  • Tooling and production planning — the factory schedules the mould, the stock, the production run. We hold the timeline.
  • Inspection — the pre-shipment QC pass. The order does not leave until it passes.
  • Freight and delivery — the consolidated shipping. We select the port, the vessel, and the delivery window.

Every gate is a place to stop. If the sample is wrong, we don't ship. If the tooling is late, we don't book the container. If the inspection fails, we rework at the factory's cost.

The brief

What we put in the brief before anything leaves your desk

Our client brief template is three pages. The first page is the product specification, the second is the timeline, and the third is the procurement notes. A complete brief looks like this:

  1. Product — SKU, dimensions, materials, finish, packaging.
  2. Reference — photo and measurements of any reference piece.
  3. Volume — units per SKU, per order, and per season.
  4. Timeline — desired ship date, order cutoff, and your client's launch date.
  5. Approval — who approves the sample, who approves inspection, who approves freight changes.

A brief without all five sections is not a brief. We return it as a checklist to the client before the factory quote is accepted.

Worked example

From selection list to first container: a practical case study

We worked with an interior brand building a residential collection. The brief: 1,800 pieces across 12 SKUs, ship to Ho Chi Minh City in August.

We selected: 4 chair SKUs, 3 table SKUs, 2 cabinet SKUs, 1 storage unit. The client had initially wanted 18 SKUs; we cut the list to 12 by removing the two pieces that had a mould cross-over risk and one piece that could not be glided to a standard bolt pattern.

Sourcing took eight weeks from brief to first container: two weeks for sample approval, four weeks for tooling and production, one week for pre-shipment QC, and one week for consolidation and freight. The client received the container with 98% of pieces passing QC on the first attempt; the remaining 2% were reworked at the factory's cost.

The result: the collection shipped on time, at a 32% margin, against a target of 24%. The collection launched two weeks early.

What to do next

Getting started with furniture selection and sourcing

If you are starting a collection or replacing an existing one, the correct first step is not a factory list. It is a one-page brief: your showroom, your customer, your space, your launch date, and your margin target.

We review that brief and tell you in two weeks whether the pieces are achievable in Foshan, and at what margin. If the selection is strong but the sourcing is weak, we take on the sourcing. If the selection is weak, we stop the process and tell you. We'd rather lose the sale than lose the margin.

We are not selling you a factory list. We are selling you a programme that gets the right furniture to the right place at the right time.
