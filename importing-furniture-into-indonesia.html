Importing Furniture into Indonesia from China: What to Know

Indonesia has its own substantial furniture export industry, particularly around Jepara's wood furniture cluster, which shapes what actually makes sense to import versus source domestically.

The main entry point

Tanjung Priok Port in Jakarta is Indonesia's primary container gateway, with ocean transit from Foshan running roughly 7 to 10 days — noticeably longer than most other Southeast Asian routes, and worth building extra lead time around.

What actually makes sense to import

Given Indonesia's strong domestic wood furniture industry, demand from China tends to concentrate on categories less commonly produced locally at scale — metal and glass office furniture, and volume contract orders for hotel groups and serviced-apartment developers.

Customs considerations

Furniture import documentation in Indonesia is detail-sensitive, and we'd recommend confirming your customs broker's specific experience with furniture HS codes before committing to a first shipment, given the added transit time already at stake if a shipment gets held up.

Building in a longer runway

Because transit time alone is close to two weeks, Indonesia-bound orders benefit from being planned with more schedule buffer than shorter regional routes — particularly for time-sensitive retail launches or project handovers.

Jakarta and Surabaya gateways

Tanjung Priok in North Jakarta is the main gateway for western Indonesia and where most Jakarta-bound furniture lands. For projects in East Java, Bali or further east, Tanjung Perak in Surabaya is often the better routing — it shortens the inland leg considerably and avoids Jakarta's road congestion entirely.

Transit from Foshan to Tanjung Priok typically runs 5 to 8 days. After discharge, allow three to five working days for clearance, and note that trucking out of Tanjung Priok is subject to peak-hour restrictions that can add half a day on their own.

Import licensing matters more here

Indonesia is the most administratively demanding of our six markets. Importers need the correct licence category, and some goods sit on the restricted list (lartas), which can require additional permits or pre-shipment inspection. Furniture is generally importable, but materials within an order — certain textiles, specific wood species, or items with electrical parts — can pull the whole consignment into a different requirement.

The practical consequence is that Indonesia rewards planning. Licences and permits take time to obtain, and unlike the other markets on this list, arriving at the port with the wrong importer category is not something a broker can fix in a day. Confirm your licensing position before you place the order.

Duty, VAT and the Form E route

Indonesia applies import duty by HS code to the CIF value, then value added tax (PPN) on the duty-paid amount, and in many cases a prepaid income tax (PPh 22) collected at the point of import. That income tax is frequently creditable against the importer's annual corporate liability rather than being a pure cost — worth confirming with your Indonesian accountant, because it changes the real landed number.

As an ASEAN member, Indonesia accepts Form E certificates of origin under the ASEAN–China Free Trade Area. The certificate must be issued before shipment and reconcile with the invoice and bill of lading; where it is missing or defective, the consignment is assessed at the standard rate and recovering the difference afterwards is slow.

Standards and knocked-down shipments

Some furniture lines fall under Indonesian national standards (SNI), and certain products require certification before they can be sold domestically — not merely imported. The other common surprise in this market is how incomplete goods are treated: a shipment arriving as loose components rather than finished furniture can be assessed differently.

If your order is being shipped knocked-down for local assembly, tell us at the planning stage so the description on the commercial documents reflects what is actually in the container.

Where first shipments usually slip

Indonesia is where paperwork discipline pays off more than anywhere else in the region. The failures we see are permits obtained after the container sailed, invoices that describe goods too loosely for the customs system, and Form E issued late. None of these are technical problems — they are sequencing problems, and they are avoidable if the document set is agreed before production finishes.
