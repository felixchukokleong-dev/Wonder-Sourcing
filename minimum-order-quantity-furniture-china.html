Minimum Order Quantities (MOQ) for Furniture Manufacturing in China

MOQ is one of the first questions any buyer asks, and one of the most misunderstood — it's rarely a single fixed number, and it usually depends on more than just the factory's stated policy.

Why MOQ exists at all

Furniture production runs in batches — a factory has to set up cutting, finishing and assembly lines regardless of order size, so a very small order can cost nearly as much per unit as a much larger one. MOQ exists to keep that setup cost from making small orders unprofitable for the factory.

Typical MOQ ranges by category

Standard case goods and dining sets often carry MOQs in the range of 50–100 units per design, while more labour-intensive upholstered pieces or fully custom designs can run higher. Simpler products with standard finishes tend to have lower MOQs than anything requiring a custom mould, fabric run or finish.

How consolidation changes the MOQ conversation

Combining several smaller product lines into one larger combined order — even across different categories — can sometimes get a factory to flex on a per-line MOQ, since the total order value still justifies their setup cost.

What to do if your order is genuinely below MOQ

Some factories will accept a smaller order at a higher unit price rather than turn away business entirely — worth asking directly rather than assuming MOQ is a hard wall. It's also reasonable to ask whether a factory has existing stock in a close specification that avoids a full production run altogether.

Per item, or per order — the distinction that matters

An MOQ quoted as a number is ambiguous until you ask what it applies to. Two hundred may be the minimum per design, meaning every chair style in your range needs two hundred units. Or it may be the minimum for the whole order, in which case the same two hundred units can be split across several designs.

The difference is enormous for anyone building a range rather than repeating one product, and it is worth confirming in writing rather than inferring from a quotation. A factory quoting per-item minimums will not necessarily refuse a mixed order — it may simply price it differently, or accept it once the total reaches a threshold.

Material minimums can exceed the furniture minimum

Some of the binding constraints are not set by the factory at all. Fabric is dyed in batches, so a supplier may have a minimum run in metres; foam comes in blocks cut to size, and an unusual density may only be sensible in volume; custom veneer, specialist glass and bespoke hardware all carry their own thresholds.

This is why a small order can run into resistance that has nothing to do with the factory's own capacity. Asking which materials in your specification carry minimums — before you negotiate, not after — usually reveals whether the constraint is real or simply a negotiating position.

Reducing MOQ without paying more per unit

Where a factory genuinely cannot run your quantity, there are honest routes. Offering to pay a setup or tooling charge covers the real cost the MOQ exists to recover. Accepting a longer lead time lets production be slotted around larger runs, which costs the factory less than it costs you. And accepting an existing finish or fabric rather than a custom one removes the material minimum entirely.

The route that looks easiest is usually the worst: pressure to cut the MOQ without addressing the cost behind it. The factory recovers it somewhere — commonly by downgrading a material or shortening an inspection — and the saving is repaid on delivery.
