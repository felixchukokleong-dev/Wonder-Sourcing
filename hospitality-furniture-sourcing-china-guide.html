Hospitality Furniture Sourcing from China: The Complete Guide

Hospitality furniture sourcing is its own discipline, distinct from retail or residential buying — driven by fixed opening dates, contract-grade durability requirements, and orders that span dozens or hundreds of identical rooms. Here's the full picture, start to finish.

Why hospitality sourcing works differently

A hotel or resort doesn't buy one sofa — it buys the same sofa two hundred times, to a deadline that doesn't move once marketing has announced an opening date. That combination of scale and fixed timing shapes almost every decision in the sourcing process, from which factory to use to how orders get inspected.

Finding the right factories for hospitality-grade production

Not every furniture factory in Foshan is set up for hospitality volume or contract-grade specification — commercial-rated foam density, reinforced joinery and fire-safety-rated fabric aren't standard on every production line. Vetting for hospitality experience specifically, not just general furniture manufacturing capability, is the first real filter.

Specifying contract grade, not residential grade

Hospitality furniture needs to survive far higher usage cycles than anything sold for a private home — this needs to be stated explicitly in a brief, since a factory quoting on a residential-grade default will price and build differently than one quoted against a stated contract-grade standard.

Planning production and freight against a fixed opening date

Because a hotel opening date is fixed, hospitality sourcing timelines get built backward from that date — production, inspection, freight and customs clearance all need enough buffer that a single delay anywhere in the chain doesn't put the opening at risk.

Inspection matters more at hospitality scale

A defect that's a minor inconvenience on a single retail order becomes a serious problem multiplied across two hundred rooms. Pre-shipment inspection on hospitality orders should be treated as a non-negotiable step, not an optional add-on.

Consolidating a multi-category hospitality order

A single hospitality project usually spans several furniture categories — casegoods, upholstery, outdoor furniture for a pool deck — often from different specialist factories. Consolidating these into one coordinated shipment is standard practice for hospitality-scale orders, and keeps both cost and delivery timing manageable.

Fire safety requirements differ by market

Flammability rules are the single most commonly missed hospitality specification, because they are market-specific and invisible in a photograph. The UK typically expects furniture to meet the relevant British Standard flammability tests for contract seating; the US market tests to California Technical Bulletin 117 or the newer TB 117-2013 depending on the item; European buyers generally ask for EN 1021 performance. Southeast Asian destinations vary instead by individual project brief and insurer.

The practical point is that "fire-rated fabric" is not a single thing. State the standard you need, by name, in the brief — a factory that is not told will default to an unrated fabric, and re-upholstering two hundred chairs after delivery is not a recoverable error.

Foam density decides how a sofa ages

Two sofas can look identical on a sample floor and behave completely differently after a year of guests. The variable is usually foam density, measured in kilograms per cubic metre, and hospitality seating should generally be specified at a higher density than residential equivalents. Lower-density foam compresses permanently under constant use, which is what produces the sagging, tired look that dates a hotel room faster than any design choice.

Ask for the density to be stated on the specification and confirmed in the pre-production sample. It is one of the few quality variables you can pin down numerically before production starts.

What belongs in a hospitality specification document

A brief that survives contact with a factory covers more than dimensions and finishes. Include the usage class (lobby versus guest room versus poolside), the foam density and fabric performance required, the flammability standard and the destination market, the frame material and joinery expectation, the packing and delivery sequence, and the quantities per room type.

Vague briefs get interpreted generously, and the interpretation that costs least is rarely the one you wanted. Writing these details down before shortlisting also makes factory quotes genuinely comparable, because every factory is pricing the same specification.
