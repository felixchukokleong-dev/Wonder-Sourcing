Restaurant & Café Furniture Sourcing from China

A restaurant's furniture has to survive nightly service, weekly deep cleans and the occasional whole-room reset — and land before the fit-out date. This guide covers bar stools, banquettes, table bases and dining chairs from Foshan, with the heights, materials and fire compliance that separate a restaurant order from a residential one.

What restaurant furniture demands that home furniture doesn't

Domestic furniture is chosen and then left alone. Restaurant furniture is sat on eight hours a day, wiped down with chemicals nightly, and dragged across floors during resets — so the spec shifts from comfort and look to usage cycles, cleanability and mass per piece. Upholstery needs to be cleanable to a stated standard and fire-compliant for the destination market; frames need to survive being tilted back on two legs by guests who will keep doing it; and anything staff move weekly should be light enough or wheeled enough that they stop lifting it.

The common trap is buying "commercial residential" — a good-looking residential chair at a good price that dies in fourteen months. The tell is in the joints and the fabric: residential frames are stapled where contract ones are corner-blocked and glued, and residential fabric carries no rub-count or cleanability certificate. Both differences cost a little per unit and a lot per replacement cycle.

The Foshan supply map for food & beverage

A restaurant fit-out usually pulls from three or four specialist factories rather than one, and the categories are cleanly separated. Steel and timber dining chairs — stackable, linkable, bar-height — run through dedicated chair workshops. Banquettes, booth seating and upholstered lounge pieces come from the Shunde upholstery studios, where contract-grade foam and vinyl production is everyday work. Table bases are their own product line, cast in iron or fabricated in steel by foundries that sell nothing else, with tops sourced separately in laminate, solid timber or stone. That separation is an advantage: you mix a base-and-top combination per venue type, and replace tops later without re-buying bases.

Dining chairs: the spec that survives service

A dining chair is judged on three things a residential buyer never has to think about: frame joints, seat cushion lifecycle, and how the whole piece behaves when stacked or linked at close. Specify corner-blocked, glued and screwed joints rather than stapled frames — a restaurant chair gets tilted back on two legs nightly, and the stapled version lets go within a year. For the seat, name a foam density (a contract cushion typically runs 32–35 kg/m³ on the seat) and an upholstery that carries a cleanability method and, where required, a fire-retardant certificate for the destination.

Stacking and linking are operations decisions, not aesthetic ones. A 120-cover restaurant that turns tables needs chairs that stack to a workable height or nest/link on a trolley; stacking chairs that are awkward to stack simply get carried two-at-a-time and get damaged. Decide the stacking count before ordering, because the stack geometry is built into the frame at the factory, not bolted on afterwards.

Bar stools and the height ladder

Bar seating fails most often on height. Get the seat height wrong for the counter and the whole run is unusable, so specify seat height and footrest separately: a counter stool is typically 610–660 mm seat height against a 1,050–1,150 mm counter, and a bar stool 710–760 mm against a 1,150 mm+ bar, with the footrest placed so a shorter guest's feet still land on it. Swivel vs fixed, back vs backless, and a footrest ring vs footplate all change comfort across a long shift.

Height is also the reason bar stools are almost never interchangeable between venues in the same brand — a café counter and a cocktail bar are different products. Order them per venue type and keep the spec sheet tied to the counter drawing, not the catalogue photo, so the next rollout of the same counter height reproduces the same stool.

Banquettes, booths and upholstery that gets cleaned

Banquette seating is where the Shunde upholstery studios earn their reputation — contract-grade foam, wipeable vinyl or performance fabric, and frames built to be fixed or freestanding as the floor plan demands. The spec that matters is the cleaning method: a nightly wipe-down with a specific sanitiser rules out untreated leather and untreated polyester, so name the surface and the cleaner together. Specify high-resilience foam and a removable, replaceable cushion cover, because the cover will be the part that needs replacing, not the frame.

Dimensions should come from the site, not a standard. Banquette depth and seat height decide both the tables-per-cover math and guest comfort over a two-hour meal, and the frame has to clear skirting, floor boxes and radiators at the back. Have the factory build to a marked-up floor plan; a "standard" banquette that is 40 mm too deep is a fit-out problem discovered on install day.

Table bases and the mix-and-match advantage

Tables are almost always a base plus a top from different factories — cast-iron or steel bases from a foundry that makes nothing else, tops in laminate, solid timber or sintered stone from the material specialists. The commercial win is decoupling them: standardise the base across a venue so it handles the wipe-down and the drag, and change tops by zone — laminate in the high-turn family area, timber in the quieter bar — without re-buying bases.

Bases fail at the column-to-base weld and the floor-contact point. Specify a fully welded column rather than a threaded one, a base plate heavy enough not to walk under service load, and adjustable feet so a slightly out-of-level floor doesn't wobble every table on it. Stable bases are the single most common silent defect in a restaurant order, and the most returned.

Fire compliance, MOQ and running the order

Food-service furniture carries fire-retardancy obligations that residential does not, and the required standard is set by the destination market — UK and EU projects typically need the upholstery and foam certified to the relevant fire standard, US contracts follow their own state and federal path. Confirm the standard before production, not at the port: a certificate the factory cannot produce after the fabric is cut is a full replacement, not a patch. Ask for the certificate per material, not per product.

MOQs run lower than office seating for the same reason chairs do — stock frames ship in the hundreds, and tops and banquettes can go lower because they are made to your dimensions. Standardise the dining chair across the venue and the numbers usually clear. Once the spec is locked, model the landed cost per cover — bulky seating dominates the freight, and the landed cost calculator turns FOB into a delivered-per-seat number so tier choices can be compared honestly. Chain rollouts reuse the same spec across sites, which is what turns single-venue MOQs into multi-site volume pricing.

Where is restaurant furniture made in China?

A fit-out usually consolidates three or four of these into one shipment.

What seat height do I need for a bar stool vs a counter stool?

A counter stool is typically 610–660 mm seat height for a 1,050–1,150 mm counter; a bar stool is 710–760 mm for a 1,150 mm+ bar. Set the footrest so shorter guests still reach it, and match the stool to the counter drawing, not the catalogue — heights are rarely interchangeable between venues.

Does restaurant furniture need fire certification?

Upholstery and foam usually do, and the required standard is set by your destination market — UK/EU projects typically need certification to the relevant fire standard, US contracts follow state and federal rules. Confirm the standard and get the certificate per material before production; one that can't be produced after cutting means full replacement.
