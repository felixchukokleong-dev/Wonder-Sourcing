Foshan Furniture Sourcing Cost Guide: What to Expect to Pay

Cost is usually the first question buyers ask about Foshan sourcing, and the honest answer is that it varies more by specification than by the fact of sourcing from Foshan itself — but there are still real patterns worth knowing before budgeting.

Why Foshan pricing isn't a single number

Foshan factories range from high-volume budget production to specialist workshops producing hospitality-grade or fully custom pieces — the same general product category can carry a wide price range depending on material, finish and order volume, more than most buyers expect before their first quote.

What actually drives FOB cost up or down

Material choice, finish complexity, hardware quality and order volume matter more than which specific factory you use — two factories quoting the same buyer on the same detailed spec tend to land in a similar range, while the same factory quoting a vague spec versus a tight one can vary significantly.

Typical cost advantage vs sourcing elsewhere in China

Because Foshan's factory density means less markup from intermediary trading offices, factory-direct Foshan pricing is often meaningfully lower than pricing obtained through a Guangzhou-based trading company reselling the same factory's product.

Budgeting total landed cost, not just FOB

FOB price is a starting point, not the number to budget a project against — freight, insurance, duties and destination handling typically add 15–35% on top, depending on product category and destination market.

The cost of buying small

Unit price falls as quantity rises, and not only because of negotiating leverage. Setup cost — interpreting drawings, building a sample, configuring a line, cutting a new die — is fixed, and the same fixed cost spread over two hundred units is a fraction of what it adds to twenty. Fabric is dyed in batches with a minimum run; foam is cut from blocks in standard sizes; both create thresholds that a small order crosses less efficiently.

The practical consequence is that a small order quoted at a high unit price is not necessarily a bad supplier. It is usually a supplier pricing the real setup it has to absorb. If the unit price matters more than the schedule, ordering fewer, larger batches rather than frequent small ones usually does more for cost than negotiating does.

The specification choices that move cost most

Within one drawing, four decisions dominate the cost: the foam density in anything upholstered, the fabric and its performance treatment, the number of finish coats and whether the finish is hand-applied, and the joinery method in load-bearing frames. Each is invisible in a photograph and each can move unit cost materially.

Where a quotation looks unusually low against others for the same drawing, the difference is almost always in one of those four. Asking the factory to state them explicitly — not to change them, just to state them — is the fastest way to find out whether two quotes are actually for the same product.

Where a cheap quote hides its saving

A low price has to come from somewhere, and the honest options are limited: less material, less labour, less process, or a willingness to make less margin. The last is real and does happen on large, repeat orders. The first three are what most low quotes actually use, and they surface later as a cushion that compresses, a joint that loosens, a finish that marks, or a carton that fails in transit.

None of that means the cheapest quote is always wrong — for a low-use decorative piece, less material may be entirely appropriate. The point is to know which of the four you are buying, which requires the specification to be written down and the quote to be itemised against it rather than accepted as a number.
