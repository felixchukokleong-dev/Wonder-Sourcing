How a China Furniture Sourcing Agent Handles Quality Control

Quality control is one of the main reasons buyers use a sourcing agent at all, but the actual mechanics of how it works — who checks what, and when — often aren't explained clearly upfront.

Independent inspection, not factory self-reporting

A sourcing agent's inspection team is salaried by the agent, not the factory — this independence is what makes the inspection meaningful, since a factory grading its own work has an obvious incentive to pass everything.

Inspection at multiple production stages

A thorough agent checks materials before production starts, construction quality partway through, and final packed goods before shipment — catching issues at each stage is significantly cheaper to fix than discovering a fault after the container has already loaded.

What a buyer actually receives

A proper inspection process produces a report with photo or video evidence and a clear pass, rework or reject recommendation — not just a verbal assurance that everything looked fine.

How disputes with the factory get handled

When an inspection fails, a good agent holds the shipment and pushes the factory to rework at the factory's own cost — the agent's ongoing relationship with that factory, not the buyer's, is what gives them leverage to make this happen.

The inspection standard, and why it is agreed in advance

An inspection that reports "some issues found" gives a buyer nothing actionable. Professional practice is to agree the standard before production begins: which attributes are checked, how many units are sampled, and how the findings are graded. Sampling normally follows a recognised statistical standard such as AQL, which sets both the sample size for a given batch and the number of permitted defects.

Findings are then graded — critical for anything structural or safety-related, major for a visible flaw on a primary surface, minor for something cosmetic on an internal part — and the batch is judged against the agreed acceptance limit rather than against perfection. Agreeing that limit in advance is what makes the result objective rather than a negotiation about how much is too much.

What a useful inspection report contains

A report is only useful if it lets someone who was not present make a decision. That means photographs of every finding, not just of the batch as a whole, with enough context to judge scale; the quantity checked against the packing list; the measurement and function checks performed; and a clear pass or fail against the agreed standard rather than a descriptive narrative.

Timing matters as much as content. A report delivered before loading is a decision point — the shipment can be held, or the finding accepted with a price adjustment. The same report arriving after the container has sailed is a complaint. The value of inspection is almost entirely in the interval between the check and the loading, which is why the schedule has to leave room for it.

Beyond inspection: controlling the process

Inspection catches problems; it does not prevent them. The higher-value control happens earlier: a pre-production meeting where the approved sample, the specification and the packing requirements are walked through with the people who will actually build the order, rather than only with the sales contact. Most avoidable defects are traceable to a factory interpreting a brief in a way nobody noticed until goods existed.

A mid-line check does the same job during production, when correcting a finish or a joint is still a matter of changing a setting rather than rebuilding furniture. Buyers who treat inspection as a single final gate tend to discover that the cheapest point to fix something is always the earliest one.
