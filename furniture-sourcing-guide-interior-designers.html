A Furniture Sourcing Guide for Interior Designers

Designers sourcing directly from Chinese factories face a specific challenge: protecting design intent through a production process built around standard specifications, not bespoke one-off pieces.

Getting design intent into a spec a factory can actually build from

A rendering or mood board communicates aesthetic intent but not manufacturing detail — a factory needs specific dimensions, material callouts and construction notes to quote and produce accurately. Working with someone who can translate a design concept into a production-ready spec sheet closes this gap.

Sample rounds protect the design, not just the buyer

For designers, a sample round isn't just quality assurance — it's the only reliable way to confirm a factory has interpreted proportions, finish and detailing the way the design intended, before committing to a full production run.

Working across multiple factories without losing consistency

A single interior scheme often needs pieces from several specialist workshops — custom joinery from one, upholstery from another. Keeping finish and material consistency across factories requires active coordination, not an assumption that everyone received the same brief.

Protecting a design from being reproduced elsewhere

Designers concerned about a custom design being replicated by the factory for other clients should raise this directly and in writing before production — not every factory will agree to exclusivity, but it's a reasonable ask worth confirming upfront.

Pricing a scheme for a client

The most common commercial mistake designers make with imported furniture is quoting a client on FOB price. FOB covers the goods to the export port and nothing after it. Freight, insurance, import duty and taxes, inland delivery into the property, and any installation labour all sit outside that number, and together they can add a substantial percentage to the figure a client has already been shown.

The workable approach is to build the landed cost with a forwarder's quotation before the client sees a price, then apply your own margin to that. It is also worth distinguishing clearly, in writing, between supply-only and supply-and-install — because the latter includes site handling, assembly and disposal of packaging, which is real work and should not be absorbed.

Specifying for the project's climate, not the showroom's

A finish that looks immaculate in a temperate showroom can behave quite differently in a tropical or coastal project. Solid timber moves with humidity; high-gloss lacquer shows every mark in strong light; untreated metal corrodes quickly in salt air. None of this is visible in a sample photograph, which is why specifying for the climate and the room's actual use is part of the design work rather than a technical footnote.

Where a project is coastal or high-humidity, it is worth stating the environment in the brief. A factory that knows the destination will specify appropriate substrates, sealants and hardware; one that assumes a temperate installation will quote a cheaper specification that fails sooner.

What to hand over when the job is finished

A design project is not complete at installation. Clients need to know how to care for what they have bought, which materials are delicate, and what to do when something is damaged. Handing over a documented specification — models, finishes, fabric references and supplier details — makes that possible and protects you from being asked to resolve a problem months later with no record of what was ordered.

Where the scheme included any custom pieces, keep the drawings and the approved sample details on file. Clients come back to extend or repair schemes, and a designer who can reorder the same item without re-sampling offers a materially better service than one who has to start again.
