Custom Furniture Sourcing: How to Order Bespoke Pieces from China

Custom furniture sourcing is the difference between being a brand and being a catalogue. Here's how to brief a Foshan factory for an original piece, control quality through prototype and tooling, and import it at a margin that actually works.

Custom furniture sourcing is what turns a reseller into a brand. A catalogue chair is a commodity; a chair with your name embossed on the back, your loft, your finish is a product. That is the difference between buying and sourcing.

At Wonder Sourcing we have guided more than a hundred brands through custom furniture sourcing. The clients who succeed are not the ones with the best-looking sketches. They are the ones who understand three things: how the factory works, what the mould actually costs, and how to inspect what doesn't exist yet.

This guide covers the full custom furniture sourcing process: from deciding whether custom is the right move, to your first import, and what to do when the first run needs rework.

OEM vs ODM vs custom

Custom, OEM and ODM: know which one you're buying

Three terms get used interchangeably, but they mean fundamentally different things:

ModelWhat it meansBest for
OEM (Original Equipment Manufacturer)You supply the design and the mould; the factory manufactures to your specs. You own the mould.Design-led brands that want control over the range.
ODM (Original Design Manufacturer)The factory designs the piece; you buy the rights. The factory has the mould already.Buyers who want unique pieces without investing in tooling.
Custom / made-to-measureThe factory builds one-off pieces from your brief, with no mould investment.Single pieces: showpiece, flagship, press pieces.

Most of our clients start with ODM and graduate to OEM as their range matures. Custom works well for press pieces and flagship showroom furniture, but it is not economical at volume.

The factory brief

How to brief a custom factory

A custom brief is not a photograph and a price. It is a document that answers five questions. We send every client home with a template we use.

  1. Design reference — a photo, sketch, or competitor piece. We ask for three angles plus a close-up of the joinery, finish or upholstery.
  2. Production specs — dimensions, frame materials, finishes, hardware. Where the spec is not fixed, we mark it as a "variable" and brief the factory to bring options.
  3. Volume — custom pieces usually start at 50–200 units. We advise clients not to commit to more than 200 until the first run passes.
  4. Finish tier — matte, satin, gloss. Each carries a materially different material cost.
  5. Launch timeline — your Q3 window. The factory needs to know if tooling can be cut in time.

The client who provides all five at the brief stage buys the best factory pricing. The client who sends a photograph and says "price it" gets the catalogue.

Tooling and moulds

Understanding the cost of custom tooling

Every custom piece that leaves a die-cast press requires a mould. The mould is the lock on the door: it is expensive to build, but it is the only thing that lets you produce indefinitely. Industry-standard mould investment runs $3,000–$8,000 for a simple design, $10,000–$25,000 for a complex two-piece design.

That investment is why we do not advise custom at scale until the first order proves the piece. The economics work like this:

  • At 50 units, the mould amortises to ~$60–$160 per unit (at full mould cost).
  • At 200 units, that drops to ~$15–$40 per unit.
  • At 1,000 units, it drops to ~$3–$8 per unit.

So the question is not "is this piece custom?". It is "at what volume is this mould worth holding?". We help clients model that before they commit to a price.

Prototype and first run

Because the factory doesn't know your brand the way you do

Custom furniture looks right on paper and wrong in reality. We have seen a mould approved by a factory as "perfect" produce a piece with furniture-grade screw holes, inconsistent edge paint, and upholstery that does not hold when the frame flexes 10mm under load.

Every custom order we manage has:

  • Prototype inspection — we bring the sample to our office in Foshan. The client sees it before tooling is cut.
  • Measurement photography — we photograph the prototype against a measured scale, not just a tape measure photo the factory emails.
  • Mock-up in the showroom — if the client is a retailer, we bring the prototype to their showroom for real lighting and real customers.
  • Finish sample approval — the client approves the finish sample, not the factory's estimate of the finish.

The first production run is always the most expensive. But the rework cost on a defective batch is significantly higher. We are prepared to hold the order and renegotiate the mould before production continues.

Importing custom pieces

From prototype to your distribution warehouse

Custom pieces follow the same freight and QC path as any other furniture order: packaging, consolidation, freight, port clearance, customs, final delivery. There is no separate route for bespoke furniture.

The two additional considerations:

  • Documentation — custom upholstery or veneer needs a material declaration. A prototype photo and a factory-issued material list let you declare the piece correctly.
  • Sample marking — we mark the prototype batch with your brand code so your QC team can trace it through to the container.

Custom pieces should be consolidated with other orders. They are typically the variety in the container that sets the scene. We always recommend sequencing: prototype run to client warehouse, then the production run consolidated.
